\begin{abstract}
  
Mutations in ATP1A3 cause a spectrum of severe neurological disorders that commonly feature episodic and stress-triggered motor symptoms, including dystonia, ataxia, and hemiplegic attacks.
ATP1A3 is the $\alpha$3 subunit of the Na$^+$/K$^+$-ATPase, which is critical for maintaining ionic homeostasis in neurons. 
However, the specific brain regions, circuit mechanisms, and physiological dynamics most vulnerable to ATP1A3 loss remain unclear, and effective treatments for ATP1A3-related disorders are lacking.
Here we generated a larval zebrafish model of ATP1A3-loss by creating a frameshift knockout of the zebrafish \emph{atp1a3a} gene, and characterized resulting behavioural and neurophysiological abnormalities to determine its suitability for disease modeling.
Mutant larvae are grossly morphologically normal, but deformation-based morphometry reveals localized differences in brain structure, and they exhibit robust disruptions in both spontaneous and stimulus-evoked behaviour, including abnormal postures and dyscoordination consistent with key clinical features.
To identify affected circuit substrates, we mapped brain-wide activity across three different stimulus and behavioural contexts, and found a common forebrain network exhibiting pronounced dysregulation, including the pallium, subpallium and habenula.
Within the subpallium, quantification of activity in individual marker-labelled neurons revealed opposing changes in the two major divisions of the basal ganglia homolog, with increased activity in the pallidal homolog and decreased activity in the striatal homolog.
Together, these findings support a model in which forebrain circuits are particularly sensitive to \emph{atp1a3a} loss mirroring clinical hypotheses, and establish larval zebrafish as a tractable platform for dissecting molecular, cellular, and circuit-level mechanisms that may drive ATP1A3-related neurological symptoms.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

ATP1A3 is the $\alpha$3 subunit of the Na$^+$/K$^+$-ATPase, which is an ATP-driven membrane pump that exports three Na$^+$ ions per two K$^+$ ions to maintain ionic gradients that set cellular excitability, and the $\alpha$3 subunit is particularly important for rapidly restoring Na$^+$ homeostasis after high-frequency firing, providing “reserve” pumping capacity during periods of high demand \citep{Holm2016-ru}.
Mutations in ATP1A3 cause a range of diseases, collectively referred to as ATP1A3-related disorders. 
These include: Alternating Hemiplegia of Childhood (AHC), Rapid-Onset Dystonia-Parkinsonism (RDP), Cerebellar Ataxia, Areflexia, Pes Cavus, Optic Atrophy, Sensorineural Hearing Loss (CAPOS), Relapsing febrile encephalopathies (RECA / FIPWE), and certain forms of early-onset disorders such as Developmental/Early-Onset Epileptic Encephalopathy (DEE), sometimes accompanied by polymicrogyria (PMG) \citep{Salles2021-bq, Li2022-wx, Vezyroglou2022-co}. 
While distinct diagnoses, these diseases share many overlapping features, including: strongly disordered movements patterns (dystonia, ataxia, etc), as well as other cognitive impairments, often affecting memory, processing speed, and executive function, as well as psychiatric manifestations such as mood disorders and anxiety \citep{Pittock2000-tq, Sweney2009-ky}. 

ATP1A3-related disorders belong to a broad class of ion-transport–mediated excitability disorders, which includes both channelopathies and disorders of ion pumps and transporters.
While this dysregulation of ionic gradients and neuronal excitability is very likely the root cause of disease, the variability among disorders associated with specific genetic alleles of ATP1A3, and the variability among patients, suggests a relatively complex and somehow specified pathology that may have differing involvement of different neurological systems \citep{Li2022-wx}.
Symptoms are often triggered or exacerbated by stress, fever, or exertion, and come in waves/attacks, indicating that the underlying disease condition is somehow transitory and correctable, or varies in severity across time \citep{Salles2021-bq}.
The brain areas, circuit- and cellular-level mechanisms that are vulnerable to ATP1A3-loss and underlie disease states, as well as these sources of symptomatic transience, are not clear.

Dystonia is a common feature of many ATP1A3-related disorders, characterized by sustained or intermittent muscle contractions that cause abnormal postures or twisting movements \citep{Albanese2013-eo}.
In RDP, dystonia is a central symptom that often has an abrupt onset in adolescence or early adulthood \citep{Haq2019-sf}.
The neurological basis of dystonia is thought to be centered in the basal ganglia (BG), which are important for refining motor programs, such as selecting appropriate actions and suppressing unwanted movements \citep{Mink2018-hi}.
Disruptions of these systems are thought to lead to excessive facilitation of motor output in dystonia and a reduced ability to suppress movements \citep{Mink2003-pf}.
However, dystonia has been increasingly understood as a disorder of distributed brain networks.
Functional imaging studies reveal abnormal connectivity between the BG, cerebellum, thalamus, and sensorimotor cortex in dystonia \citep{Vo2023-sq}.

Pharmacological manipulations of the cerebellum in animals can produce dystonic movements \citep{Calderon2011-no}, and patients with cerebellar disease may also develop dystonia \citep{Alarcon2001-gx}.
Ataxia, which is characterized by a loss of coordinated, accurate movements, often arises from cerebellar dysfunction \citep{Kuo2019-ei}, and is also often observed in ATP1A3-related disorders, thus further implicating the cerebellum as a core locus of vulnerability in ATP1A3-related disease. 
In addition to dystonia and ataxia, episodic hemiplegia is a defining feature of AHC, reflecting transient, lateralized paralysis.
Such symptoms seem incompatible with focalized lesions or dysfunction. 
Instead, hemiplegia points to dynamic dysfunction of distributed motor networks and impaired control of neuronal excitability.

Here we have generated a larval zebrafish model to better understand the role of ATP1A3 in neuronal circuits and to gain insights into potential cellular and circuit underpinnings of ATP1A3-related disorders.
We generated a frameshift knockout of the zebrafish \emph{atp1a3a}, and characterized its behavioural and neurophysiological abnormalities.
These mutants exhibit strong and widespread disruptions in both spontaneous and stimulus-evoked behaviour, some of which mirror clinical features of ATP1A3-related disorders, including aberrant postures, dyscoordination and phenotypic transience.
Whole-brain functional imaging identified a common network in the forebrain that consistently shows dysregulated activity patterns including areas of the pallium, subpallium and habenula.
Collectively, our data support a model in which forebrain circuits are particularly sensitive to ATP1A3-loss, and lay the foundation for using the larval zebrafish model to study the molecular, cellular and circuit-level mechanisms underlying forebrain-derived neurological symptoms in these diseases.


\section{Results}
\subsection{A larval zebrafish model of ATP1A3-related disorders.}
\subsubsection{Generation and characterization of a null mutants allele of atp1a3a}

Due to the teleost genome duplication, zebrafish possess two genes that are orthologous to human ATP1A3: \emph{atp1a3a} and \emph{atp1a3b}. Zebrafish \emph{atp1a3a} has 21 exons and shares 92\% amino acid conservation with human ATP1A3, and a similarly broad gene expression in neurons throughout the central nervous system \citep{Doanl2013}. 
The \emph{atp1a3b} gene also shows very high conservation (91\%), but its expression is highly localized to certain brain areas in the midbrain and hindbrain \citep{Doanl2013}. 
For this project have focused on the more highly-conserved and more broadly-expressed \emph{atp1a3a} gene. 
We generated a mutant line using CRISPR-Cas9, yielding an 8 base pair frameshifting deletion in exon 8, and premature stop codon in exon 9 (\autoref{fig:1}A). 
qRT-PCR experiments show a strong loss of \emph{atp1a3a} mRNA levels, indicating that the transcript is degraded through nonsense-mediated decay (\autoref{fig:1}B).
We tested for genetic compensation through paralog upregulation \citep{Rossi2015}, and indeed found that \emph{atp1a3b} mRNA levels are significantly higher in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, indicating that \emph{atp1a3b} overexpression may compensate for \emph{atp1a3a} loss. 
Therefore, while we expect the \emph{atp1a3a} allele to be completely non-functional, this may be partially compensated for by both the presence and upregulation of the \emph{atp1a3b} paralog.

Previous experiments manipulating \emph{atp1a3a} in the zebrafish larvae have led to inconsistent and contrasting findings, and therefore it was unclear whether a zebrafish mutant could be a useful model for ATP1A3-related disorders. 
Firstly, morpholino-based knockdown of \emph{atp1a3a} and \emph{atp1a3b} found brain ventricle dilation, as well as depolarization of the Rohon-beard neurons and motility deficits \citep{Doanl2013}.
However, in a more recent study, Cas9-generated mutants for these genes did not exhibit  morphological abnormalities in the brain or ventricles, but did exhibit behavioural hyperactivity and insomnia \citep{Barlow2023}.
Overexpression of human ATP1A3 mRNA, or a mutated form associated with RDP and AHC (p.Ala275Pro) resulted in reduced brain size, reduced touch-evoked escape responses, and hypoactivity phenotypes \citep{Ruan2024}.
While such inconsistencies may relate to the differential methods employed, the relatively mild behavioural phenotypes in presumed-null mutant lines \citep{Barlow2023}, make it unclear if larval zebrafish would recapitulate any of the more dramatic clinical symptoms of ATP1A3-related diseases, such as dystonia and ataxia.

\subsubsection{Localized differences in brain structure in atp1a3a mutants}

As the studies described above disagree as to whether ATP1A3-loss alters brain structure in zebrafish, we sought to address this question quantitatively.
It is not merely a descriptive one: severely impaired Na\textsuperscript{+}/K\textsuperscript{+}-ATPase pump function can disrupt brain morphogenesis in humans, and a subset of ATP1A3 variants cause developmental and epileptic encephalopathy accompanied by polymicrogyria or progressive brain atrophy \citep{Vetro2021}, with ATP1A3 expressed in the developing human cortex from fetal stages \citep{Smith2021}.
Our mutants appeared grossly normal, but gross inspection is a blunt instrument for detecting the kind of localized differences that such a process might produce.

We therefore used deformation-based morphometry to compare brain structure quantitatively, using the Jacobian determinants of the non-linear transformations that register each brain to the reference \citep{Randlett2015}.

This revealed significant differences in a restricted set of brain areas (\autoref{fig:1}C,E).
Differences were predominantly, though not exclusively, in the direction of reduced volume in mutants, and were concentrated in midline diencephalic structures -- the ventral and dorsal thalamus, rostral and intermediate hypothalamus, posterior tuberculum and preglomerular complex -- as well as in the olfactory bulb and olfactory epithelium.
Against this background, a smaller number of regions showed locally \emph{increased} volume in mutants, the most prominent of which within the brain was the telencephalic pallium.
Importantly, these differences were regionally restricted rather than brain-wide: the majority of Z-Brain atlas regions showed no significant difference in either direction, which is not the pattern expected if mutant brains simply differed from wild-types by a uniform change in overall size.
The same comparison performed between mutants and their heterozygous siblings gave concordant results, with the great majority of regions showing effects in the same direction in both comparisons.

To assess whether this analysis is prone to detecting spurious structural differences, we performed the identical comparison between \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/-}} and \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/+}} siblings, which were reared, fixed, stained, imaged, registered and analysed in parallel (\autoref{fig:1}D).
This comparison returned essentially no signal, with all but one Z-Brain region showing no significant difference in either direction, and the total signal being more than two orders of magnitude smaller than in the comparison of mutants to wild-type siblings.
Heterozygous larvae are therefore structurally indistinguishable from wild-types by this measure, consistent with the largely recessive nature of the behavioural phenotypes described below, and indicating that the differences we detect in homozygous mutants are not an artefact of the registration or analysis pipeline.

\subsubsection{atp1a3a mutants show abnormal postural phenotypes and dyscoordination}

Our \emph{atp1a3a} mutants also appeared morphologically normal, except for deflated swim bladders in some larvae, which is often associated with behavioural abnormalities as inflation requires coordinated swimming to the surface to gulp air. 
Consistent with this hypothesis, visual observation in 6-7dpf larvae confirmed that they often exhibit abnormal swimming patterns and postures, this primarily manifested as "unbalanced" swimming, where larvae failed to maintain consistent dorso-ventral alignment while swimming and instead swayed from side to side, and were often observed in abnormal postures like laying sideways on the bottom of the dish, and postures with the tail persistently bent to one side of the body (\autoref{fig:2}A-B, \autoref{videosupp:transience1}A). In more extreme cases, larvae lost balance completely and swam in a corkscrew motion, or tight circular swimming patterns.
As these phenotypes are reminiscent of the abnormal postures and dyscoordination observed in patients with ATP1A3-related disorders, we sought to characterize these phenotypes quantitatively.  
We found that nearly all WT \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/+}} larvae displayed normal swimming behavior (96\%), while the majority of \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{-/-}} fish were abnormal, displaying either an unbalanced swimming (69\%) or bent tail (18\%) phenotypes.
A significant portion of heterozygous \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/-}} larvae were categorized as abnormal (16\% unbalanced, 1\% bent tail), but the majority were indistinguishable from WT. 
The distribution of phenotypes in \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/+}}, \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/-}}, and \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{-/-}} larvae was significantly different (Fisher-exact test, p < 0.001, n=55, 138, 106 for \textsuperscript{+/+}, \textsuperscript{+/-} and \textsuperscript{-/-}, respectively). These results indicate that the CRISPR induced mutation leads to a variety of abnormal behavioural phenotypes and that there is a degree of haploinsufficiency in the heterozygous condition, but homozygous mutants show more severe phenotypes, consistent with a loss-of-function mutation in \emph{atp1a3a}.

\subsubsection{Phenotypic transience in atp1a3a mutants}

A key feature of ATP1A3-related disorders is that symptoms often come in waves/attacks, and can be triggered by stress, fever, or exertion \citep{Salles2021-bq}. This was also the case in our mutants, where we observed that fish could transition between abnormal/unbalanced swimming, and normal swimming phases. 
For example, a mutant larva exhibiting strong unbalanced swimming is swimming normally 3 minutes later in the same recording session (\autoref{videosupp:transience1}).
To quantify the frequency and prevalence of these transitions, we repeatedly observed fish from heterozygous incrosses in 24-well plates every hour over 3 hours, and then again 5 hours later, categorizing them as "Normal", "Mild Unbalanced", or "Severe Unbalanced" (\autoref{fig:2}C), and then did post-hoc genotyping to identify their mutational status. 
While wildtype fish were always classified as normal, \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{-/+}} and \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{-/-}} showed very strong dynamics in their phenotypic expression, with nearly all fish that we observed in an "unbalanced" state transitioning between categories across observations (\autoref{fig:2}C).

As the tail-bending phenotype was difficult to quantify in this multi-well plate format, we used a head-embedded preparation in which the larva is embedded in agarose with the tail free to move, and presented visual stimuli to elicit swimming behaviour using a projector and screen below the larvae (\autoref{fig:2}D).
Observing \emph{atp1a3a} mutants in this setup revealed periods of normal swimming interspersed with periods of persistent tail bends, with the tail often stably bent to one side for many seconds (\autoref{fig:2}E).
Tail tracking and swimming kinematic analyses \citep{Randlett2023} confirmed that WT sibling fish exhibit the typical bouting swimming pattern, with variable levels of activity in different fish, but that the tail angle remains stably centered along the midline (\autoref{fig:2}F).
In contrast, \emph{atp1a3a} mutants show a very bursty swimming pattern, with vigorous bouts of swimming followed by periods of quiescence. 
Dramatically, these larvae show many periods of persistent lateral tail bending reflected as large deviations in the tail angle from the midline, lasting many seconds or minutes (\autoref{fig:2}E-G).

Based on these data we conclude that the loss of \emph{atp1a3a} results in quite dramatic postural abnormalities in larval zebrafish, and that these phenotypes appear to often be transient in nature, paralleling this key feature of the human ATP1A3A-related disorders.

Having established that \emph{atp1a3a} mutants exhibit complex behavioural abnormalities via categorical observation, we next sought to more systematically characterize their phenotypes under spontaneous conditions, and to identify potential underlying neural circuit dysfunctions. 
For this purpose, we tracked their behaviour in a multi-well plate setup under normal lighting conditions, in which 300 larvae can be observed simultaneously (\autoref{fig:3}A) \citep{Randlett2019-fj,Lamire2023-he}.
Consistent with previous results \citep{Barlow2023}, we found that \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{-/-}} larvae display hyperactivity, characterized by increased bout frequency, increased median displacement per bout and increased median bout duration (\autoref{fig:3}B-D).
Therefore, mutants are hyperactive and not only initiate more movement bouts, but also move in larger bouts.
Under these conditions, we did not observe significant phenotypes in heterozygous \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/-}} larvae, indicating that such gross movement phenotypes may be more specific to the homozygous condition, or that the heterozygous condition has more subtle, transient, or less penetrant phenotypes that are not revealed by these population-wide analyses.

However, this hyperactivity was specific to the multi-well format.
When we analysed spontaneous swimming in the absence of any stimulus in the much larger channels used for our optomotor response assay (20~mm $\times$ 104~mm, \autoref{fig:3}E), \emph{atp1a3a} mutants were instead clearly \emph{hypo}active, initiating movement bouts at less than half the rate of their wild-type and heterozygous siblings (\autoref{fig:3}F).
The kinematics of the bouts that mutants did perform were affected in the same direction as in the multi-well assay, with modestly increased displacement per bout and bout duration (\autoref{fig:3}G,H), so the reversal is specific to how often larvae initiate movement rather than to how they move once they start.
We do not know which feature of the two arenas is responsible, but they differ in the space available to the larvae, in the lighting, and in the handling required to load them (see Methods).
This context-dependence is worth emphasizing, because it means that whether these mutants appear hyperactive or hypoactive depends on the assay in which they are observed, and it cautions against treating either result as a general description of the mutant.

In order to identify potential neural substrates and brain areas involved in these abnormal swimming phenotypes, we mapped the brain-wide activity of \emph{atp1a3a} mutants relative to their WT siblings using the MAP-Mapping approach \citep{Randlett2015}. 
MAP-Mapping uses immunostaining of phosphorylated ERK (pERK) relative to total-ERK (tERK) levels as a proxy for neuronal activity, combined with image volume registration and statistical analysis to identify regions with altered pERK/tERK ratios.
We analyzed cohorts of larvae that were left to swim freely in a 10cm petri dish in a brightly lit environment, and determined group genotyping post-imaging. 
pERK/tERK ratios were compared voxel-wise to generate a brain-wide statistical map of altered activity under these baseline behavioural conditions (\autoref{fig:3}I).

Based on the widespread expression of \emph{atp1a3a} in the brain, as well as the very strong and broad electrophysiological phenotypes expressed in other ATP1A3-deficient models, we expected to see very widespread alterations in neural activity across many brain areas.
In contrast, MAP-Mapping revealed a distributed but fairly restricted pattern (\autoref{fig:3}I).
Consistent with the hyperactivity or perhaps dysregulated motor coordination in these mutants, we saw significant activation in some motor-areas, including the spinal cord and midbrain tegmentum, while the cerebellum showed areas of both increased and decreased activity.
The most prominent alterations in pERK/tERK signals were seen in the forebrain, including the pallium, subpallium and habenula. 
The potential relationships with these altered forebrain activity patterns and hyperactivity is not clear, but do indicate that these areas of the brain appear to be particularly sensitive to \emph{atp1a3a} loss, and thus may be critical for understanding the circuit-level mechanisms underlying the behavioural phenotypes in these mutants.

To test the stimulus-response behaviour of \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, we began with our standard 300-well screening assay (\autoref{fig:4}A), in which larvae are exposed to trains of "Dark Flash" stimuli (DF, a sudden transition to darkness that initiates the O-bend response) \citep{Randlett2019-fj,Lamire2023-he}. 
Stimuli are delivered at 1 minute intervals, and the response probability and kinematics are quantified for each stimulus across the population of larvae.
This revealed that \emph{atp1a3a} mutants show a very strongly reduced probability of executing an O-bend response to the DFs, indicating that they may be mostly blind to this stimulus (\autoref{fig:4}B). 
However, visual inspection of the responses to DFs revealed that many mutants did appear to respond, but did so with a reduced amplitude of bending (\autoref{fig:4}C). 
This reduced amplitude bending does not meet our criteria for an "O-bend" response, defined as a turn with a minimal bend amplitude of 3 radian, and thus such responses are categorized as "non-responses" by default \citep{Randlett2019-fj}.
When we relaxed these criteria to consider any turning response within the 1-second of the DF stimulus, we found that the response probability of mutants was much more similar to siblings (\autoref{fig:4}D), demonstrating that mutants are mostly still able to respond to the stimulus, and only show a significantly reduced probability of responding in the initial phase of the assay (\autoref{fig:4}Ei-iii), but do so with a strongly reduced bending amplitude (\autoref{fig:4}Fi-iii).
This may either reflect a reduced ability to execute large amplitude bends, or perhaps that the perceived strength of the stimulus is reduced in mutants, leading to a reduced motor response.


To gain insight into the neural substrates underlying the reduced responses to DFs, we performed MAP-Mapping analysis of brain-wide activity patterns in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants relative to WT siblings after being stimulated with 10 DFs at 1 min intervals (\autoref{fig:4}G).
This revealed a very strong suppression of activity in the optic tectum, which is the largest primary visual area in the zebrafish brain, and shows strong responses to DFs in WT larvae \citep{Lamire2023-he}. This indicates that the visual DF response is suppressed early in the visual pathway, suggesting sensory deficits early in the circuit. 
Suppressed responses were also observed in the cerebellum, and similar to what we observed in the spontaneous condition, we saw relatively subtle alterations in hindbrain areas. Again, the most striking alterations were seen in the forebrain, including the pallium, subpallium and habenula, which show strong patterns of both activation and suppression relative to WT siblings (\autoref{fig:4}G).

\subsubsection{The C-Bend escape response to acoustic stimuli}
To test a different sensory modality and motor-response, we used acoustic/tap stimuli (a brief vibrational stimulus delivered with a solenoid rod that initiates the C-bend response) \citep{Burgess2007-2}. In contrast to the reduced responses to DFs, \emph{atp1a3a} mutants showed an increased response probability to acoustic/tap stimuli (\autoref{fig:4}Bi, Di, Eiv), with a bend amplitude indistinguishable from controls (\autoref{fig:4}Fiv). 
Therefore, \emph{atp1a3a} mutants are not simply hyporesponsive to stimuli, but exhibit very different effects in different assays, indicating a differential role for Atp1a3a in different circuit contexts.

\subsubsection{Induced turning and swimming in the optomotor response (OMR)}
The optomotor response is an intensively studied visuomotor behaviour in which larvae respond to whole-field motion stimuli with directional swimming responses \citep{Portugues2009}. 
To test the optomotor response in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, we placed larvae in an arena in which they can swim freely in rectangular channels while being presented with either global red light (No-Stimulus condition) or a striped pattern moving along the tracks alternating between upwards and downwards motion (OMR condition) (\autoref{fig:5}A). 

We quantified the response of larvae to this motion in three ways: 
\\1) increased swimming  (\autoref{fig:5}B), 
\\2) turning towards the direction of motion, which is visible as a realignment of the mean orientation of the larvae towards the axis of stimulus motion within a few seconds of motion onset (\autoref{fig:5}C),
\\3) the OMR-following performance of the fish calculated as the proportion of swimming in the dimension following motion (up or down) relative to the total motion of the fish (\autoref{fig:5}D).

Interestingly, \emph{atp1a3a} mutant larvae showed a robust increase in swimming during the stimulation period, indicating that they are able to perceive the stimulus and respond with increased swimming activity (\autoref{fig:5}B). 
However, they showed a significantly reduced turning response to the direction of motion, with a much smaller realignment of the orientation of the larvae towards the axis of motion of the stimulus (\autoref{fig:5}C). 
Mutants also showed a reduced increase in vertical swimming relative to horizontal swimming (\autoref{fig:5}D), indicating a reduced ability to use the motion cues to guide their swimming direction.
We note also that mutants showed hypoactivity relative to WT siblings in the "No-Stimulus" condition (\autoref{fig:5}B, p=0.049, Kruskal-Wallis/Dunn's), consistent with the reduced rate of spontaneous bout initiation that we observed in these same channels in the absence of any stimulus (\autoref{fig:3}F), and opposing what we found when tracking their behaviour in our multi-well assay (\autoref{fig:3}B). As discussed above, this hypoactivity may be related to the larger arena, different lighting, or differing handling during the experiment (see Methods).

We again then used MAP-Mapping analysis to determine the brain-wide activity patterns in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants relative to WT siblings after being stimulated with an OMR stimulus (\autoref{fig:5}E).
Similar to the DF stimulus (\autoref{fig:4}G), we observed a strong suppression of activity in the optic tectum -- though in a clearly distinct more posterior/ventral pattern, reflecting the differential topographic presentation of the OMR stimulus below the animals. 
We observed relatively little change in the diencephalic pretectum, which is thought to be more critical than the tectum for motion processing in the context of the OMR \citep{Matsuda2021-a, Wang2020-a}, indicating that this area may be less affected in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants.
However, activity was strongly suppressed in multiple areas of the anterior hindbrain (Rhombomeres 1/2), which has been associated with OMR sensorimotor processing \citep{Chen2018-sdf}, as well as turn direction regulation through the ARTR \citep{Dunn2016-asd}. 
Therefore, the reduced OMR turning responses in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants may be related to dysfunction in these areas. 
Similar to the DF stimulus, we observed strong patterns of both activation and suppression in the forebrain, including the pallium, subpallium and habenula, again consistent with a model in which these areas are particularly sensitive to \emph{atp1a3a} loss irrespective of stimulus or behavioural context.

Our behavioural and neural imaging experiments revealed that \emph{atp1a3a} mutants show dramatic behavioural alterations across various different contexts, including postural problems, hyper- and hypo-activity, reduced responses to visual stimuli, and increased responses to acoustic/tap stimuli. 
Based on this limited sampling of behavioural phenotypes, it is difficult to determine how these different phenotypes may relate to one another, and whether they are related to dysfunction in the same or different brain areas and circuits.
In our MAP-Mapping experiments focusing on three stimulus contexts (No Stimulus, Dark Flashes and OMR), we observed some expected altered activity patterns in known areas, such as the optic tectum and hindbrain, that could explain some of the stimulus-specific phenotypes.
However, some of the most dramatic patterns were in the forebrain, and appeared to be highly consistent across experiments: including the pallium, subpallium and habenula. 

To examine the consistencies in these patterns quantitatively, we directly compared the MAP-Mapping results across these three experiments by generating an intersection map, where only pixels that show significant alterations in all three experiments are retained (\autoref{fig:6}A).
This revealed a very striking and bilaterally symmetrical pattern of altered activity, with a zone of co-activation in the habenula (HB\textsuperscript{A}), activation of the lateral pallium (Pa\textsuperscript{A}), and two zones of activation and suppression in the subpallium (SPa\textsuperscript{A} and SPa\textsuperscript{S}, respectively).
We noted that the pallial zone of activation (Pa\textsuperscript{A}) falls within the region in which the morphometric analysis identified locally increased volume (\autoref{fig:6}B, compare \autoref{fig:1}C), making the pallium the one region in which both measures are altered in the same location; we return to the interpretation of this coincidence in the Discussion.

The zebrafish habenula is thought to function as a key integrative hub linking sensory inputs and internal state to downstream neuromodulatory circuits, thereby regulating aversive processing, stress coping, attention, and action selection \citep{Okamoto2021}. 
Distinct dorsal and ventral habenular subdivisions contribute differentially to these processes, with the dorsal habenula modulating innate and state-dependent behaviors and the ventral habenula supporting associative learning.
Therefore, altered habenular activity in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants may contribute to the altered stimulus responses and hyperactivity phenotypes through dysregulated sensory processing.

The pallium and subpallium are the major divisions of the telencephalon, where the pallium is homologous to the mammalian cortex and contains predominantly excitatory neuronal populations, while the subpallium comprises conserved telencephalic domains homologous to the mammalian basal ganglia, septal nuclei, and components of the extended amygdala, giving rise predominantly to inhibitory neuronal populations involved in action selection, motivation, and emotional processing \citep{Wullimann2004}.
Dystonia and other ATP1A3-related neurological disorders are widely thought to arise, at least in part, from dysfunction within basal ganglia circuits, which play a central role in motor control, action selection, and the regulation of movement vigor \citep{Brggemann2021}. 
We were therefore particularly interested in examining basal ganglia–associated structures to assess whether the altered subpallial activity is occurring within these conserved motor circuits.

Tanimoto et al., have recently defined markers for the basal ganglia homologs in zebrafish based on conserved gene expression and connectivity analyses \citep{Tanimoto2024}, which we used to operationally define the larval zebrafish basal ganglia subregions in our activity maps. 
In this framework, \emph{tac1} and \emph{penkb} are used as markers of Vd/Vc, the homologs of the striatum, while \emph{npy} and \emph{nkx2.1} demarcate the dorsal entopeduncular nucleus (dEN), the homolog of pallidum. 
We stained for \emph{tac1}, \emph{penka}, \emph{penkb} and \emph{npy} using HCR in situ hybridization, and registered these images to the Z-Brain atlas \citep{Randlett2015} (\autoref{fig:6}C-F, \autoref{videosupp:basalganglia1}).
This revealed that the zone of suppressed activity in the subpallium (SPa\textsuperscript{S}) is proximal to markers for the Vd/Vc, the homolog of the striatum, while the zone of activated activity (SPa\textsuperscript{A}) overlaps with the dEN, the homolog of the pallidum (\autoref{fig:6}C).
This led us to the hypothesis that \emph{atp1a3a} loss results in opposing changes in activity within the two major divisions of the basal ganglia homolog: increased activity in the pallidal homolog and decreased activity in the striatal homolog, a pattern that would be consistent with the known inhibitory connectivity among these regions.
However, these comparisons rest on the spatial correspondence between activity maps and marker gene expression after registration to a common atlas, and are therefore limited by the precision of this registration and by the fact that the activity maps are computed across, rather than within, individual cells.
Two zones that appear proximal or overlapping in atlas coordinates may nonetheless correspond to distinct populations of neurons.

To test this hypothesis directly at the level of individual neurons, we combined HCR labelling of \emph{npy} and \emph{penkb} with pERK immunostaining in the same larvae, and quantified the pERK signal within individual marker-labelled neurons (\autoref{fig:6}G).
Larvae were genotyped after imaging, allowing us to compare \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{-/-}} larvae with their \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/+}} siblings processed and imaged in parallel.
This confirmed the predictions of the atlas-based analysis: \emph{npy}-labelled neurons of the dEN/pallidum homolog showed significantly increased pERK signal in mutants (\autoref{fig:6}H), while \emph{penkb}-labelled neurons of the Vd/Vc striatum homolog showed significantly decreased pERK signal (\autoref{fig:6}I).
Therefore, the opposing activity changes we observed in the subpallium are occurring within the marker-defined basal ganglia populations themselves, and not merely in adjacent territories that happen to lie nearby in atlas coordinates.
This provides direct cellular-level evidence for a model in which basal ganglia circuits are particularly sensitive to ATP1A3-loss, with the pallidal and striatal homologs affected in opposite directions.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Zebrafish larvae as models for complex movement disorders like ATP1A3-related disorders}

Larval zebrafish are a leading model for genetics and systems neuroscience, but as a very immature and rapidly developing non-mammalian vertebrate, there may be some fundamental limitations to their use as models for human neurological diseases.
For this reason, it is still somewhat unclear to what extent they will be truly useful in modeling movement disorders like ATP1A3-related disorders, which are thought to arise from dysfunction in complex forebrain structures, the cerebellum, or in a very distributed and perhaps human-specific way.
In particular, the promise of larval zebrafish modelling lies is both the ability to approach systems neuroscience in a comprehensive way, and to take advantage of the high-throughput nature of the model to screen for genetic and pharmacological modifiers of disease phenotypes.
But, how are we to know if the phenotypes we observe in a larval zebrafish disease model are relevant to the human disease, and whether they can be used as a basis for further mechanistic and therapeutic studies?
It may not be possible to answer this question \emph{a priori}, but the first and most relevant step is a thorough analysis of the behavioural phenotypes present in the model. 
Ideally, this can also be complemented and extended by neurological analyses to determine loci of physiological modification in the brain. 
These data can then be compared to human or mammalian model-system phenotypes (if such data is available), to determine if the phenotypes manifesting in larval zebrafish are indeed relevant and likely mechanistically related to those observed in disease states in humans.

Perhaps the best "proof of concept" for the relevance of larval zebrafish neurological disease modelling comes from Dravet syndrome, where \emph{scn1lab} mutant larvae show spontaneous seizure-like behavior and epileptiform brain activity, and they often show poor response to sodium-channel blockers, thus showing high phenotypic similarity to the human condition \citep{Baraban2013-NC, Dinday2015-EN}. 
Large-scale larval screens identified clemizole and later lorcaserin as seizure-suppressing compounds that act, unexpectedly, via serotonergic pathways. 
This has led to clinical trials and motivating human use of serotonergic agents, including fenfluramine, which has been approved as a clinical treatment \citep{Lagae2019-LS, Nabbout2020-JAMA}. 


\subsection{The behavioural phenotypes of \emph{atp1a3a} mutants are complex and context-dependent} 

Based on previous work with morpholinos and \emph{atp1a3a/b} mutants, it was unclear to what extent we might be able to model ATP1A3-related disorders in larval zebrafish.
Previously reported phenotypes varied widely and included brain ventricle malformation, stimulus insensitivity, or more mild effects like hyperactivity and insomnia. 
Our more comprehensive behavioural phenotyping of \emph{atp1a3a} mutants has revealed a much more complex and nuanced picture, where mutants show a range of different phenotypes across different assays, including postural problems, context-dependent hyper- and hypo-activity, reduced responses to DF stimuli, a specific reduction in directional responses to OMR stimuli, and increased responses to acoustic/tap stimuli.
Directly relating these phenotypes to the human symptoms like dystonia, parkinsonism, hemiplegia, and ataxia is not straightforward, but the presence of postural problems and other motor phenotypes that are context-dependent and show both hypo- and hyperactivity, as well as altered responses to sensory stimuli, is consistent with the complex and variable nature of symptoms in human patients.

Perhaps the clearest parallels between what we have observed in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants and the human disease condition is the presence of postural problems.
While this awaits a more comprehensive understanding of the neurological basis, the "unbalanced swimming" may be understandable as a model of ataxic behaviour.
The "bent tail" phenotypes, where the fish adopt an abnormal and sustained posture with the tail bend to one side for many seconds, could be related to dystonic postures, or perhaps hemiplegic postures.
This could either reflect a sustained lateralized contraction, or conversely, lateralized paralysis.
Future physiological analyses of the motor circuits and muscular electrophysiology will be required to determine the exact nature of these postural problems, and how they might relate to the human symptoms.

Symptomatic transience is one of the major hallmarks of ATP1A3-related disorders, where patients often show periods of normal or near-normal function interspersed with periods of extremely severe symptoms, which can be triggered by specific sensory stimuli or stressors. 
Symptoms can also be relieved by sleep, and thus somehow show a strong dependence on internal states or their transitions. 
We have shown that \emph{atp1a3a} mutants also show transience in their phenotypes, with periods of normal swimming interspersed with periods of unbalanced swimming and bent-tail postures. 
We have not yet explored the possible triggers or relievers of these phenotypes. 
There are many established stressors used in larval zebrafish, ranging from mechanical stimulation, light changes/strobing, temperature/heat-shock, and chemical stressors such as salt, cadaverine, or alarm pheromone. 
We note that the "bent tail" phenotypes that we observe in head-embedded larvae appear to be much more extreme and frequent than those we observe in free-swimming larvae, which are often more subtle and intermittent. 
The head-embedding process itself is a strong stressor, and the inability to move and a lack of visual feedback motion triggers a passivity/freezing response that is triggered by noradrenergic neurons in the  locus coeruleus, which in turn trigger activity waves in large populations of radial glia cells initiating this passivity response \citep{Mu2019}.
While comparing directly the behaviour of the animals in head-embedded vs. freely swimming preparations is difficult, it is possible that the stress of this embedding process is sufficient to trigger waves of attacks in these larvae. 
If so, the behavioural context of the preparation may be an important determinant of the severity of the phenotypes observed, both behaviourally and physiologically.
If future analyses are able to identify specific and reliable stressors that can trigger the postural phenotypes in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, this may provide a model for understanding the mechanisms of symptom transience in ATP1A3-related disorders, and for screening for drugs that can prevent or relieve these catastrophic symptoms.

\subsection{Understanding the circuit-level pathologies in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants and their implications for disease modeling}

We have used pERK-based MAP-Mapping to analyze brain-wide activity patterns in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, which provides a proxy for neuronal activity across the whole brain with regional resolution and a slow integrative timecourse of $\approx$15 minutes before fixation \citep{Randlett2015}, complemented by quantification of pERK signal within individual marker-labelled neurons to resolve activity changes at the cellular level.

\subsubsection{Consistent alterations in forebrain activity across different stimulus contexts}

As has been done in the context of other neurological disease models, such as autism and schizophrenia \citep{Thyme2019_cell, WeinschutzMendes2023_CR}, we used MAP-Mapping as a first-pass method to identify the brain areas that show altered activity in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants. 
This allows one to rapidly generate hypotheses as to what circuit dysfunctions exist in a model, and where across the brain.
We did this first in the absence of any overt stimulus, when the fish were "freely swimming" in the light. 

Based on the known physiology of ATP1A3, and the strong motor phenotypes we observed that show some parallels to epileptic models like PTZ treatment \citep{Baraban2005_JN}, we expected to see very strong and widespread alterations across many brain areas, perhaps reflecting unconstrained excitation and epilepsy. 
We were therefore quite surprised to see that the most of the brain showed very little change in activity. 
From this we concluded that the average mutant brain appears to be relatively normal physiologically, with only isolated areas showing significant alterations in activity (under these conditions and with limitations of this method). 
This is consistent with the fact that these mutants show relatively normal swimming behaviour for much of the time, and thus the "averaged" response may not show very strong alterations in brain activity under these baseline conditions.

While it is true that much of the brain appeared normal in our MAP-Maps, a strong pattern of altered activity was seen in the forebrain, including the telencephalon (pallium and subpallium) and habenula. 
These areas, and especially the telencephalon, are not typically directly associated with sensorimotor processing in larval zebrafish, but are more associated with higher-order sensory processing, internal state regulation, and action selection.
However, we believe that this pattern of altered forebrain activity may be critical to the altered states observed in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, because they were consistently observed across different stimulus contexts, including the presentation of DF and OMR stimuli.

DF and OMR stimuli elicit different patterns of altered activity in other areas such as the optic tectum and hindbrain, but the same telencephalon/habenula pattern was observed.
This suggests that the forebrain is particularly sensitive to \emph{atp1a3a} loss, and that dysfunction in these areas may be a common underlying factor contributing to the various phenotypes observed in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants across different contexts. 
However, this potential causal relationship will need to be tested in future experiments, for example by using optogenetic or chemogenetic methods to manipulate activity in these areas and determine how this affects the phenotypes observed in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants.
This potentially prominent role for the forebrain in driving phenotypes in this mutant is particularly interesting given that the forebrain, and in particular the basal ganglia, are widely thought to be key loci of dysfunction in ATP1A3-related disorders.  

Structures like the "basal ganglia" are difficult to identify concretely in the larval zebrafish brain, which has not developed the elaborated and more nucleated structure of the adult. However, gene expression patterns can be used to define sub-domains and cell-type territories within the telencephalon \citep{Pandey2023_GR}, and we attempted to determine if the areas we identified as dysregulated in the subpallium might be homologous to the basal ganglia using atlas-based alignment of such marker genes \citep{Tanimoto2024}. This analysis showed very coherent overlap between the activation patch in the ventro-lateral subpallium and \emph{npy}, which is the marker for the pallidum/dEN. We also used markers for the striatum homologs (Vd/Vc, marked by \emph{tac1}, \emph{penka}, \emph{penkb}) to determine if the zone of suppressed activity in the medial subpallium overlaps with these areas. This analysis was less conclusive as the marker genes were very proximal but appeared to be directly adjacent to the zone of suppression, leaving it unclear whether the suppression was occurring within the striatum or in an adjacent area.
This ambiguity is inherent to atlas-based approaches, which compare group-averaged activity maps to marker expression after registration to a common reference brain, and which therefore cannot resolve whether a given change in activity occurs within the marker-expressing cells themselves.

We therefore turned to a within-animal, single-cell approach, measuring pERK signal directly within individual \emph{npy}- and \emph{penkb}-labelled neurons in the same larvae.
This confirmed both predictions of the atlas-based analysis, with increased pERK signal in the pallidal homolog and decreased pERK signal in the striatal homolog of mutants relative to their wild-type siblings.
This resolves the ambiguity above: the suppression we observed does occur within \emph{penkb}-expressing neurons of the striatal homolog, rather than in a neighbouring territory.
The fact that these two populations are affected in opposite directions is notable, as it argues against a simple global change in neuronal activity or in pERK levels in the mutant forebrain, and instead points to a specific reorganization of activity within basal ganglia circuitry.
Such an imbalance between striatal and pallidal activity is of particular interest given that dystonia and other ATP1A3-related symptoms are widely thought to arise from altered signalling through these pathways \citep{Brggemann2021}.
We note, however, that pERK provides an integrated and very indirect measure of activity over a timescale of minutes and that these measurements were made in a single behavioural context, so the extent to which this imbalance is stable or itself fluctuates with the transient phenotypes described above remains an open question.



\subsubsection{Basal ganglia and cerebellum as loci of dysfunction in ATP1A3-related disorders}

Which brain structures are responsible for generating dystonia in ATP1A3-related disorders has been a central and contested question, and the most influential experimental answers have come from pharmacological mouse models of RDP.
Using localized ouabain infusion to partially block sodium pumps, Calderon et al. concluded that the primary instigator of dystonia was the cerebellum, and that aberrant cerebellar output altered basal ganglia function through a di-synaptic thalamic pathway, which when severed alleviated the dystonia \citep{Calderon2011-no}.
Subsequent work in the same model showed that partial block of sodium pumps restricted to the cerebellum is both necessary and sufficient to induce dystonia, and that Purkinje cells are especially sensitive to sodium pump dysfunction, which disrupts their intrinsic pacemaking and results in erratic high-frequency burst firing \citep{Fremont2014}.
Together with converging evidence from other dystonias, these findings have shifted the field substantially towards the cerebellum, to the point that the basal ganglia are no longer regarded as the sole, or even the primary, locus of dysfunction \citep{Tewari2017}.

Our observations in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants are complementary to this view, and point back towards the basal ganglia.
The cerebellum was certainly altered in our mutants, showing zones of both increased and decreased activity under spontaneous conditions (\autoref{fig:3}I) and suppression following DF stimuli (\autoref{fig:4}G).
However, when we asked which alterations were common to all three of our stimulus and behavioural contexts, the cerebellum did not survive this intersection, whereas a bilaterally symmetrical forebrain signature did (\autoref{fig:6}A).
Within the subpallium, the affected populations proved to be the marker-defined homologs of the striatum and pallidum themselves, altered in opposite directions (\autoref{fig:6}G-I).
To our knowledge this is the first demonstration in an intact, behaving vertebrate that genetic loss of \emph{atp1a3a} produces an activity imbalance between the two major divisions of the basal ganglia, and it indicates that the sensitivity of these circuits to ATP1A3-loss is not a mammalian peculiarity but is conserved in a vertebrate whose lineage diverged from ours over 400 million years ago.

Two caveats are important here.
First, our measurements are correlative, and we have not tested the hierarchy between structures in the way that the mouse work has.
Our data are therefore equally compatible with a model in which the subpallial changes we observe arise downstream of cerebellar dysfunction, which is precisely what the cerebello-thalamo-basal ganglia pathway would predict \citep{Calderon2011-no, Tewari2017}.
Second, the cerebellum of a 5-6 dpf larva is still developing and is far simpler than its mammalian counterpart, so its absence from our intersection map should not be read as evidence that the cerebellum is spared in ATP1A3-related disease.
What our data do establish is that basal ganglia circuitry is measurably and specifically dysregulated in this model, which makes the larval zebrafish a tractable system in which the causal relationship between these two structures could be dissected -- for example by combining whole-brain activity imaging with targeted ablation or optogenetic manipulation of cerebellar output while monitoring subpallial activity and postural phenotypes.

The third structure retained in our intersection map, the habenula, deserves comment, not least because it is the component of our forebrain signature that requires no interpretive argument about homology: unlike the subpallial territories discussed above, the habenula is a directly and unambiguously homologous structure across vertebrates.
It is also not as disconnected from the basal ganglia result as it might first appear.
In mammals, the lateral habenula receives its major forebrain input from the entopeduncular nucleus, the same structure whose zebrafish homolog (dEN) contains the zone of increased activity we identify in the subpallium.
Co-activation of the dEN homolog and the habenula in the same intersection map is therefore consistent with engagement of this conserved pathway, rather than representing two unrelated observations.
Consistent with this, the lateral habenula has been repeatedly, if sporadically, implicated in animal models of dystonia, generally as a recipient of altered basal ganglia output: altered glucose utilization is seen in the lateral habenula of the dystonic rat mutant \citep{Brown1989} and in a primate model of dopamine agonist-induced dystonia \citep{Mitchell1990}.
Most relevant to our results, in the genetically dystonic \emph{dt\textsuperscript{sz}} hamster -- in which dystonic attacks occur spontaneously or in response to mild stress -- habenular c-fos induction is indistinguishable from controls at baseline, but fails to increase normally when animals are stressed \citep{Ebert1996}.

We should be clear that we are not aware of any previous work linking the habenula to ATP1A3 or to ATP1A3-related disorders specifically, and our data cannot establish such a link.
We would, however, tentatively raise one hypothesis that we think is worth testing.
The defining clinical feature of ATP1A3-related disorders is that symptoms are episodic and are precipitated by stress, fever or exertion \citep{Salles2021-bq}, and the habenula is a conserved hub for aversive processing, stress coping and the control of behavioural state transitions \citep{Okamoto2021}, with experimental disruption of habenular development in zebrafish sufficient to elevate cortisol and anxiety-like behaviour \citep{Facchin2015}.
It is therefore conceivable that habenular dysfunction contributes to the trigger-dependence and transience of symptoms rather than to the motor phenotype itself, which would place the habenula and the basal ganglia homologs in complementary roles in this model.
We emphasize that this is speculation: our measurements were made in animals that were not subjected to any deliberate stressor, we did not manipulate the habenula, and we have no evidence bearing on whether habenular activity differs between the normal and abnormal behavioural states that individual mutants transition between.
Testing it would require measuring habenular activity across these transitions, and asking whether stressors that are known to engage this system alter the probability of the postural phenotypes.
We also note that the dorsal habenula of larval zebrafish is strongly left-right asymmetric \citep{Facchin2015}, and that a more targeted analysis than the one we present here would be needed to determine whether the alterations we observe respect this asymmetry.

\subsubsection{Comparison with an independent \emph{atp1a3a} mutant line}

While this work was in progress, \citet{Moyer2026} reported a screen of zebrafish mutants for schizophrenia-associated genes that included independently generated \emph{atp1a3a} alleles -- a protein-truncating allele and a point mutation corresponding to the human ATP1A3 V129M variant identified in childhood-onset schizophrenia -- as well as an \emph{atp1a3b} mutant.
Their analysis used the same pERK-based activity mapping and atlas registration framework that we use here, which makes the two datasets directly comparable, and the agreement between them is substantial.
Most importantly, they report strongly increased activity in the lateral telencephalon of the protein-truncating \emph{atp1a3a} mutant, against a background of reduced activity in most other brain regions.
This is the same signature we observe, in an independently generated allele, in a different genetic background and in a different laboratory, and it is reassuring that the forebrain locus we identify is not specific to our allele or our conditions.
Their behavioural findings also overlap with ours, including increased duration and length of individual swimming bouts and a reduced probability of responding to dark flashes.
In addition, they quantify seizure-like movements in the protein-truncating mutant and report impaired performance in a juvenile Y-maze working memory assay, phenotypes that we did not assess and that extend the range of behavioural abnormalities associated with loss of this gene.

There are also differences between the two studies that are worth considering.
First, \citet{Moyer2026} report reduced brain size in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, whereas we observe a mixture of localized volume decreases and increases, and specifically an increase rather than a decrease in the pallium.
We note that these conclusions are drawn from the same class of measurement, and that their reported reduction reflects a structural comparison in which most brain regions differed in the same direction, which is the pattern expected when brains differ in overall size.
Our own comparison also shows a predominance of volume decreases, and we would not argue that mutant brains are unaffected in overall size; rather, the analyses differ in whether they are dominated by a global effect or resolve regional differences within it, and this may simply reflect differences in how overall brain size is treated in the two pipelines.
The two results are therefore less discrepant than they first appear, and we would emphasize the regional decreases on which the two studies agree, including the olfactory bulb and diencephalic structures.
Second, they report that acoustic responses were relatively normal in both \emph{atp1a3a} lines and that the marked acoustic phenotypes were instead seen in \emph{atp1a3b} mutants, whereas we find increased responsiveness to acoustic stimuli in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants.
Given the differences in assay design, stimulus intensity and analysis between the two studies, we do not think this discrepancy can be resolved with the data currently available.

Finally, their observation that \emph{atp1a3b} mutants show stronger behavioural phenotypes than either \emph{atp1a3a} line, despite weaker effects on brain activity and structure, is relevant to the compensation issue we raise above.
It indicates that the two paralogs are not simply redundant, and that their relative contributions differ between brain-wide physiology and behavioural output.

\subsubsection{What might be going wrong in the \emph{atp1a3a} mutant telencephalon?}

Our data localize the most consistent physiological alterations in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants to the telencephalon and habenula, and show that within the subpallium these changes occur within the marker-defined basal ganglia populations themselves.
What they do not yet reveal is what is going wrong within these circuits at the level of neuronal activity.
One possibility that we think deserves particular attention relates to the quasi-epileptic phenomena associated with ATP1A3-loss, including the cortical spreading depolarizations that have been observed in mouse models of ATP1A3-related disorders and are thought to be related to the paroxysmal symptoms \citep{Hunanyan2014, Balestrino1999}.
Spreading depolarization is a slowly propagating wave of near-complete neuronal depolarization followed by prolonged synaptic silencing \citep{Dreier2011}.
Because recovery from spreading depolarization critically depends on Na$^+$/K$^+$-ATPase activity, ATP1A3 dysfunction is predicted to lower the threshold for its initiation and prolong recovery \citep{Hunanyan2014, Balestrino1999}.
This framework accounts for the defining features of hemiplegic episodes, including abrupt onset, reversibility, sleep-related resolution, and alternating laterality across attacks.

Whether events of this kind occur in the \emph{atp1a3a} mutant telencephalon, and whether they coincide with the transitions between normal and abnormal swimming that we observe behaviourally, is not addressed by the measurements reported here: pERK integrates activity over a timescale of minutes, and our measurements were made in animals fixed in a single behavioural context without knowledge of their phenotypic state at that moment.
Directly recording neuronal activity in these areas across the transitions between phenotypic states is therefore, in our view, the most important next step for this model, and would establish whether the paroxysmal nature of ATP1A3-related symptoms has a tractable physiological correlate in larval zebrafish.
Should this prove to be the case, the high-throughput nature of the larval zebrafish would make it possible to screen for interventions (such as high-oxygen treatment, \citealp{Papadopoulou2023_MD}) that suppress these events.

A second, and not mutually exclusive, possibility is suggested by our morphometry.
The pallium is the one brain region in which we detect both a local increase in volume (\autoref{fig:1}C,E) and consistently increased activity across stimulus contexts (\autoref{fig:6}B), and this coincidence invites the question of whether the two are related.
There are several reasons to be cautious in interpreting it.
Both measurements derive from the same registrations, and local volume differences can in principle produce apparent intensity differences in voxel-wise activity maps by concentrating or diluting signal.
We think this is unlikely to account for our results, for three reasons: pERK signals are normalized to tERK within the same tissue, so that a pure concentration effect would largely cancel; the subpallial populations in which we quantified activity directly at the level of individual neurons (\autoref{fig:6}G-I) show no detectable volume difference, so those measurements cannot be explained in this way; and in the habenula the two measures run in opposite directions, with decreased volume accompanied by increased activity, which is the opposite of what a concentration artefact would produce.
Nevertheless, we cannot exclude a contribution of this kind in the pallium specifically.
If the pallial volume increase is genuine, its cellular basis remains open: it could reflect a difference in cell number, cell size or neuropil, an activity-dependent structural change, or cell swelling arising from the impaired capacity of mutant neurons to maintain ionic and therefore osmotic gradients.
The last of these is attractive because it would follow directly from the known function of the pump and because neuronal swelling is a feature of spreading depolarization, but it is speculation.
We note that this question is directly addressable with the material we have already generated, since counting marker-labelled neurons within the affected region would distinguish a difference in cell number from a difference in the volume occupied by a similar number of cells.
More broadly, the existence of a localized structural phenotype in the cortical homolog of a null mutant is consistent with a role for the $\alpha$3 subunit in forebrain morphogenesis, as indicated by the polymicrogyria and progressive atrophy seen in patients carrying severely loss-of-function ATP1A3 variants \citep{Vetro2021, Smith2021}, although the larval zebrafish pallium is not laminated in the manner of the mammalian cortex and we would not claim to model that malformation.

\subsection{Conclusions}

Our behavioural and neurological analyses of \emph{atp1a3a} mutant zebrafish larvae have revealed a complex and nuanced picture of the phenotypes associated with ATP1A3-loss in this model system, which we believe are relevant to the human disease condition and can be used as a basis for further mechanistic and therapeutic studies.
This model we have generated is a simple loss-of-function model, while all of the patient-associated mutations that have been identified to date are heterozygous point mutations.
These alleles may have more complex and perhaps more dominant effects on the function of the protein or the Na\textsuperscript{+}/K\textsuperscript{+}-ATPase itself, and thus may not be fully captured by a simple loss-of-function model.
Most of the phenotypes we observe in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants are recessive, and so this inheritance pattern is a major distinction between our model and the human disease. However, in this project we have completely ignored the potential role of the \emph{atp1a3b} paralog, which is also expressed in the brain and may have some functional redundancy with \emph{atp1a3a}. 
This is very probable, as the previously reported hyperactivity/insomnia phenotypes were seen in both \emph{atp1a3a} and \emph{atp1a3b} mutants \citep{Barlow2023}, and we observed overexpression of \emph{atp1a3b} in \emph{atp1a3a} mutants.
Thus the presence of this paralog may be sufficient to prevent the manifestation of phenotypes in heterozygous \emph{atp1a3a} mutants.

Therefore, it will be critical to analyze \emph{atp1a3b} mutants and \emph{atp1a3a;atp1a3b} double mutants, as well as point mutation models carrying patient-associated alleles, which may show different phenotypes and may be more relevant to the human condition.
Progress on both fronts has recently been made by \citet{Moyer2026}, who characterized an \emph{atp1a3b} mutant alongside their \emph{atp1a3a} alleles, and generated a point mutation corresponding to the human ATP1A3 V129M variant.
Their finding that the \emph{atp1a3b} mutant shows stronger behavioural phenotypes than either \emph{atp1a3a} allele, while showing weaker effects on brain activity and structure, argues that the two paralogs make distinct rather than simply redundant contributions, and makes the double mutant a particularly informative experiment for the future.
Notably, their point mutant produced milder phenotypes than the protein-truncating allele, which is consistent with our expectation that the relationship between allele type and phenotype severity in this gene is not straightforward.
Such efforts will be facilitated by the very high conservation of the ATP1A3 protein, where all of the most frequently detected mutations reside in a conserved amino acid in zebrafish (e.g. AHC: p.D801N, p.E815K, p.G947R; RDP: p.T613M, p.I758S; CAPOS: p.E818K; RECA: p.R756H/C/L).

These efforts will work towards the goal of understanding the cellular and circuit nature of ATP1A3-related disorders symptoms/phenotypes, and in particular their paroxysmal nature, which is one of the most debilitating and least understood aspects of these disorders. Our ultimate goal is to not only use the larval zebrafish as a model for understanding the mechanisms of these disorders, but also to use it as a high-throughput platform for screening for drugs that can prevent or relieve the catastrophic symptoms of these disorders, which are currently largely untreatable.



\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Animal husbandry and care}
All experiments were carried out using larval zebrafish between 5 and 6 days post-fertilization (dpf), maintained in E3 embryo medium supplemented with 0.02\% HEPES (pH 7.2) at a density of approximately 1 larva per mL.
Larvae were housed in 10 cm petri dishes under a 14:10 h light/dark cycle at a constant temperature of 28-29°C.
Zebrafish were bred and maintained at the SickKids Zebrafish CORE facility (Toronto, Canada) and the Animalerie Zebrafish Rockefeller (AZR, SFR Santé Lyon Est, Lyon).
All housing and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national regulations.
French facilities were approved by the French Ministry of Agriculture and the Ministry of Higher Education and Research (Agreement \#C693870602) and overseen by the local ethics committee (comité d’éthique en expérimentation animale de la Région Rhône-Alpes: CECCAPP).
Experiments conducted in Canada were approved by the University of Toronto Animal Care Committee, in accordance with guidelines established by the Canadian Council for Animal Care (CCAC).


\subsection{Generation of the \emph{atp1a3a} mutant line}
The \emph{atp1a3a} CRISPR mutant zebrafish line was generated at the SickKids Zebrafish CORE facility (Toronto, Canada).
The guide RNA (gRNA) was designed to target the \emph{atp1a3a} gene 82 base pairs downstream of the start of exon 8.
This specific region was chosen due to its close proximity to two human mutations known to cause Rapid-onset Dystonia Parkinsonism (821T>C mutation and 829G>A mutation).
The resultant \emph{atp1a3a} mutant allele has an 8 base pair deletion in exon 8 leading to a frameshift mutation with a premature stop in exon 9.
For Ca\textsuperscript{2+} imaging experiments, \emph{atp1a3a} mutants were crossed with \emph{Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6f); mitfa\textsubscript{-/-}}, to generate a line that express the nuclear-targeted GCaMP6f Ca2+ indicator pan-neuronally, in the pigment deficient \emph{Nacre} mutants.


\subsection{Genotyping}
Larvae were lysed in 50 mM NaOH at 95°C for 10 min and neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) in a 1:9 volume ratio (Tris:NaOH), to extract genomic DNA.
Target region of atp1a3a was amplified using FIREPol® Master Mix Ready to Load 1× (Solis Biodyne) and gene-specific primers at 0.1 \textmu M.
Primer sequences used were: Forward : CGGTATTGTGGTCTGCACCG, Reverse: GCCGGTGATGATGTGGATGA.
Wild-type alleles yielded amplicons of 119 bp, while mutant alleles are 111 bp, which were distinguished on an 3.5\% agarose gel.




\subsection{Analyses of Spontaneous Behaviour}
The spontaneous behaviour of larvae was recorded using a high-speed imaging system adapted from BonZeb-based kinematic tracking \citep{Guilbeault2021}.
The setup included a Sentech STC-CMB200PCL CameraLink camera (Aegis Electronic Group, Inc.) with a zoom lens (Kowa), connected via a GrabLink Full XR Frame Grabber (Euresys Inc.) for acquisition of 1088 × 1088 resolution frames at 332 Hz.
Larvae were placed on a 6 mm thick transparent polycarbonate platform, illuminated from below by an 850 nm LED plate.
A 1920 × 1080, 60 Hz DLP pico projector (P7, AAXA Technologies Inc.) was integrated into the system, with projected light directed onto the platform using a cold mirror (Knight Optical).
Real-time video acquisition and processing were carried out in the Bonsai programming environment \citep{Lopes2015}, which interfaced with BonZeb for automated tracking \citep{Guilbeault2021}.
This configuration was used to obtain free-swimming recordings of \emph{atp1a3a} mutants, from which subsets of frames were extracted in FIJI and z-projected to generate composite images illustrating abnormal phenotypes and swim sequences.
Free swimming was further analyzed at 5 dpf using the 300-well plate setup we have previously described, where larvae are recorded in 8mm x 6mm (diameter x depth) wells \citep{Lamire2023-he}. 
Larvae were recorded over a 30-minute period in the absence of visual and acoustic stimulation.
From these recordings, the mean displacement per bout (mm), mean bout duration (s), mean peak bout speed (mm/s), total displacement (mm), and bout frequency (Hz) were calculated using custom python scripts (available at \href{https://github.com/owenrandlett/2025_atp1a3a}{github.com/owenrandlett/2025\_atp1a3a}).

\subsection{Analyses of the Optomotor Response (OMR)}

The OMR was assessed in 5dpf larvae in an acrylic arena containing 3 separate corridors allowing fish to swim in a 10.4cm x 2cm arena.
Larvae were subjected to alternating vertically moving OMR gratings using a projector (AAXA LED Pico Projector) and a 45° cold mirror.
OMR gratings were calculated and dynamically displayed using the “PsychoPy” package in python \citep{Peirce2007}
The gratings ran continually for 20 seconds epochs, alternating between directions along the long-axis of the arena, spanned by a 10 second long "no-stimulus" interval.
In total, the alternating OMR stimuli ran for 10 minutes, subjecting each larva to a total of 20 repeats of the stimuli.
Fish were tracked using IR LED arrays, and a camera (Chameleon 3 USB3 FLIR) running at 30 frames per second with an IR filter, and custom machine-vision scripts (available at \href{https://github.com/owenrandlett/2025_atp1a3a}{github.com/owenrandlett/2025\_atp1a3a}).
Lighting was projected at a screen under the arena using a projector (AAXA LED Pico Projector) and a 45° cold mirror.


\subsection{Analyses of the Dark Flash Response}
Larval behavior was assessed in custom 300-well acrylic plates (8 mm diameter, 6 mm depth; ~300 µL water volume) based on a modified version of previously described setups.
The plates were positioned beneath a 31\degree C water bath, which served as a heated lid to prevent condensation and maintain the wells at 29\degree C.
Recordings were captured using a Mikrotron CXP-4 camera (500 Hz) connected to a Silicon Software frame grabber (Marathon ACX-QP, Basler) and illuminated with IR LEDs (TSHF5410, digikey.com).
Visual stimulation was provided by a rectangular array of 155 WS2813 RGB LEDs, with dark flashes (DFs) consisting of a 1 second light-off period followed by a linear return to baseline over 20 seconds.
Acoustic/vibration taps were delivered via a solenoid (ROB-10391, Sparkfun).
The experimental design alternated 1 hour of stimulation with 1 hour of rest, during which a stepper motor-driven actuator (Hanpose HPV4, 500 cm) moved the camera between two plates, enabling up to 600 larvae to be tested simultaneously.

Control of the LEDs, solenoid, and camera actuator was managed by a Raspberry Pi Pico running CircuitPython and custom Python software, which also handled video acquisition and online tracking of head and tail positions at 20-30 Hz.
During DF or tap delivery, a 1 second high-speed burst recording was captured and later analyzed offline using background subtraction and morphological operations.
Tracking and analysis relied on open-source libraries, including OpenCV, scikit-image, NumPy, SciPy, and Numba.


\subsection{MAP-Mapping Analyses of Brain-Wide Activity Patterns}
Whole-brain activity mapping was performed using a MAP-mapping pipeline adapted from Randlett et al. (2015).
Cohorts of 150-200 larvae produced from adult atp1a3a+/- ; mitfa-/- ; Tg(elavl3:Huc-GCaMP6f) incross were subjected to visual stimuli as following:
For free swimming, larvae were grouped in one 10 cm petri dish and left swimming in a lit environment for 40 minutes in the same behavior setup as was used for the OMR stimulation (described below) to ensure identical external conditions.
For OMR stimulation, larvae were grouped in one 10 cm petri dish, left to acclimatize for 10 minutes then subjected to concentric gratings that alternated vertically each 30 seconds, using the OMR behavior setup.
For dark flash stimulation, larvae were grouped in one 10 cm petri dish, left to acclimatize for 20 minutes then subjected to 10 dark flashes following the dark flash protocol and using the dark flash habituation setup.
Larvae were then fixed and processed for immunohistochemistry with anti-pERK (Cell Signaling Technology, \#4370S) and anti-tERK primary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, \#4696S), followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies: Alexa FluorTM 647 for tERK (Thermo Fisher Scientific, \#A21235) and Alexa FluorTM 568 for pERK (Thermo Fisher Scientific, \#11036).
Brains were imaged in dorsal orientation on the Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope equipped with a 25× water-immersion objective (HC FLUOTAR L 25x/0.95 W VISIR, CIQLE imaging platform, Lyon).
Image stacks were acquired at a voxel resolution of 0.08 × 0.08 × 2 µm, and two partially overlapping volumes were tiled to encompass the entire brain.
The GCaMP6f channel was used as an anatomical reference for nonlinear registration of each specimen to a standardized reference brain via the Computational Morphometry Toolkit (CMTK).
Registered image volumes were subsequently down-sampled to 300 × 679 × 80 voxels (x, y, z) and spatially smoothed using a two-dimensional Gaussian filter.
To account for inter-individual variability in staining intensity and morphology, voxel-wise pERK signals were normalized to corresponding tERK values prior to statistical comparison across experimental conditions.

\subsection{Deformation-based morphometry}
Local differences in brain structure were assessed from the same registered image volumes used for MAP-Mapping, following the approach described in \citet{Randlett2015}.
For each specimen, the Jacobian determinant of the non-linear transformation mapping that brain onto the reference brain was computed at every voxel, giving a voxel-wise measure of local expansion or contraction required to bring the specimen into register.

Jacobian values were compared voxel-wise between genotypes, and significant differences were summarised within the anatomical regions of the Z-Brain atlas \citep{Randlett2015} by summing the significant differences within each region.

\subsection{HCR in situ hybridization and quantification of pERK signal in marker-labelled neurons}
Larvae from \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/-}} incrosses were raised to 5dpf, fixed, and processed for hybridization chain reaction (HCR) in situ hybridization \citep{Choi2018} using probe sets against \emph{npy}, \emph{penka}, \emph{penkb} and \emph{tac1} (a gift from Herwig Baier's laboratory), following [MANUFACTURER/PROTOCOL] with hairpins conjugated to [FLUOROPHORES].
HCR labelling was the followed with anti-pERK immunostaining as described above, so that marker gene expression and pERK signal were acquired from the same specimens.
Larvae were imaged in dorsal orientation on the Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope with a 25$\times$ water-immersion objective (HC FLUOTAR L 25x/0.95 W VISIR, CIQLE imaging platform, Lyon) at a voxel resolution of [XYZ RESOLUTION].
Following imaging, each larva was genotyped individually as described above, allowing \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{-/-}} larvae to be compared with \emph{atp1a3a\textsuperscript{+/+}} siblings that had been fixed, stained and imaged in parallel.

\subsection{Data analysis}
Phenotypic distributions were visualized and analyzed using multiple statistical approaches.

Pie charts depicting the relative proportions of phenotypes in atp1a3a mutants were generated with Python packages Matplotlib, Pandas, and NumPy.

The same visualization pipeline was employed for phenotype transience assays, while histograms of tail angle distributions were constructed using these packages as well.

Statistical evaluation of swim kinematics during OMR assays was conducted with the Pingouin library.

Outliers were identified as values exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range below the first quartile or above the third quartile and were excluded prior to subsequent analyses.

For RT-qPCR, relative expression levels of atp1a3a and atp1a3b were quantified using the $\Delta\Delta Ct$ method.

Data were analyzed with one-way ANOVAs followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc t-tests.

Outlier replicates were defined as those with |z| > 3.5 and removed prior to analysis.

A threshold of p < 0.05 was adopted to determine statistical significance in all tests.

Data analysis was carried out in Python using a custom-developed script.

Behavioral events were operationally defined based on cumulative tail bend angle thresholds, with deflections greater than 3 radians classified as O-bends and those exceeding 1 radian designated as C-bends.

The computational framework integrated several open-source libraries: NumPy, Pandas, Numba and SciPy for statistical procedures.

Data visualization was performed using Matplotlib and seaborn.

Statistical comparisons between distributions were conducted using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test as implemented in SciPy.
